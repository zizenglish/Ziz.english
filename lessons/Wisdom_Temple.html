Chapter II · The Hall of the Storyteller

Your Brain Is Making It Up

Imagine a film editor who has to finish the film before it is shown, and has no time to ask the director what happens next. That editor is your brain. It guesses, patches and smooths so that the world feels continuous and complete.

Find your blind spot

  1. Cover your left eye.
  2. Look at the cross with your right eye. Do not look at the circle.
  3. Start about 20 cm from the screen and move slowly closer, then back.

At one distance the circle vanished, and the screen did not look broken. Where the nerve leaves your eye there are no light-sensing cells, so there is a hole in your vision. Your brain fills in the gap with the background around it and you never notice.

Fact or myth?

Your eyes send a complete, sharp picture of the world to your brain.

Each of your eyes has a blind spot that you normally never notice.

When you see something, you are seeing what is happening at this exact moment.

A question for you

If your brain fills in so much, how much of what you “see” every day is actually there, and how much is a good guess? Give your honest estimate and explain it.

Chapter IV · The Courtyard of Change

The Beam That Was Replaced

So far we have doubted what we see. Now let us doubt something even more basic: what it means for anything to stay the same.

The temple keeper replaces one old wooden beam every year. After forty years, not a single original beam remains. Is it still the same temple?

Now make it personal

Think of something in your own life that has changed completely but still has the same name: a city, a friendship, a job, even you. In what sense is it still “the same”? Is that comforting or unsettling?

Chapter V · The Hall of Debate

How to Doubt Well

Wise people do not simply say “you are wrong”. They question gently and precisely. Here are five expressions that let you disagree, hesitate and probe in a way that sounds confident and thoughtful.

I’m not entirely convinced that…a polite, firm way to express doubt“I’m not entirely convinced that speed is the same as progress.”
up to a pointpartly, but not completely“Seeing is believing, up to a point.”
for all we knowwe have no evidence against this possibility“For all we know, the person next to you sees blue as you see green.”
It strikes me that…a thoughtful way to share an observation“It strikes me that we trust our eyes far more than we should.”
That can’t be the whole storythere must be more to it than what has been said“Brains guess; fine, but that can’t be the whole story.”

Chapter VI · The Road Beyond the Temple

Keep Exploring

The best part of any journey is the road home with something new in your head. Here is your companion for the week.

Podcast · Clearer Thinking with Spencer Greenberg

The Range of Human Perceptual Experiences

A conversation with neuroscientist Anil Seth. I chose it because it follows directly from today. He explains why perception is closer to a “controlled hallucination” than a recording of reality, and why some people can see sounds, imagine nothing in their minds or hear voices. If you enjoyed the blind spot and the apple, you will love this one.

Listen or read the transcript

The page includes a full transcript, so you can read along or check any phrase you miss.

Questions to chew on

Teacher notes · not for the student

Teacher Notes

Close this panel before screen sharing again. About 60 to 75 minutes with a C1 learner.

Timing

  • Chapter I: 10 minutes · Chapter II: 12 · Chapter III: 10 · Chapter IV: 12
  • Chapter V: 15 (gap fill 4 minutes, take-a-stand 10) · Chapter VI: 3 to introduce the homework

Answers

  • Müller-Lyer: both lines are equal. Let the student commit before pressing “Measure them”.
  • Fact or myth: myth, fact, myth.
  • Gap fill: 1 taken in, 2 jump to conclusions, 3 a matter of perspective, 4 clung to, 5 for all we know, 6 up to a point.
  • Chapter III experiences: all three are real; there is no wrong choice.
  • Temple beam: no correct answer. Push the student to justify with the expressions.

Facilitation tips

  • Let each textarea answer be spoken first, then typed if you want written practice.
  • After the blind spot, ask: “What else might your brain be filling in right now?”
  • In Chapter V, feed back on the quality of the doubt (precision, tone), not only the grammar.
  • Note two errors and two strong phrases from the student’s talk for delayed correction at the end.
  • If the student is quick, add: “Now argue against your own opinion for 30 seconds.”
