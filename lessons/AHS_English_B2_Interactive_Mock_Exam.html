Reading Task 1 · Multiple choice

Questions 1–5

Learning beyond the classroom

When people talk about education, they often imagine a classroom, a teacher standing in front of a board and students taking notes. Yet learning does not stop when the school bell rings. More and more schools are encouraging students to take part in projects outside the traditional classroom, from volunteering in local communities to working with scientists or businesses.

Supporters of this approach argue that practical experiences help young people understand why school subjects matter. A student who has studied biology may find it easier to understand an ecosystem after helping to restore a local pond. Someone learning mathematics might discover its value while planning the budget for a school event. These activities can also develop skills that are difficult to practise through written exercises alone, such as teamwork, communication and problem-solving.

However, learning outside school is not automatically successful. A poorly organised project can become little more than an enjoyable day away from lessons. Students need clear goals, preparation and time to reflect on what they have learned. Teachers also need to make sure that every student can participate, regardless of their family income or personal circumstances.

There is another question: should practical projects replace traditional lessons? Most educators say no. A strong education needs both knowledge and experience. Students need facts and theories before they can use them effectively in real situations. The challenge is to connect the two, rather than treating them as competing approaches.

Reading Task 3 · Short answers

Questions 11–15

Read the text and answer each question in no more than FIVE words.

The quiet rise of repair cafés

In many towns, people are beginning to question the habit of throwing away broken objects. Repair cafés offer a simple alternative. Visitors bring items such as lamps, bicycles, toys or small electrical devices, and volunteers help them repair what they can. The idea is not to provide a free professional service. Instead, it is to share practical knowledge and encourage people to understand how everyday objects work.

The movement has social benefits too. People who might never normally meet sit together, exchange skills and talk. Older visitors may share experience with younger people, while students often bring digital knowledge that older volunteers find useful. Organisers say that the atmosphere is as important as the repairs themselves.

Of course, not everything can be fixed. Some devices are designed in ways that make repair difficult, and certain electrical problems require qualified professionals. Repair cafés therefore focus on safe, manageable repairs and explain when an expert is needed. Their wider message is that buying something new should not always be the first solution.

Why urban green spaces matter

City parks are sometimes described as attractive extras, but research and everyday experience suggest that they perform several important functions. Trees provide shade, plants can help absorb rainwater, and green areas offer habitats for birds and insects. During hot periods, neighbourhoods with more vegetation may feel cooler than areas dominated by concrete and traffic.

Green spaces can also support people's well-being. A short walk in a park may offer a break from noise and screens, while shared gardens can bring neighbours together. Nevertheless, access is not equal in every city. Some districts have large parks nearby, while others have very few public green areas. This has led to calls for urban planning that considers environmental quality alongside housing and transport.

Creating a park is only the beginning. Green spaces need regular care, suitable facilities and safe paths. Local residents should ideally be involved in decisions about how these places are designed, because different communities may have different needs.

Speaking Task 2 · Comparing pictures

2–3 minutes

Imagine two photographs: one shows teenagers studying together in a modern library; the other shows a teenager learning a practical skill outdoors with an instructor.

Compare the situations. Say what the young people might be learning, how the experiences may be different and which type of learning you would prefer. Explain why.

Useful language: Both pictures show… / Whereas… / It seems as though… / They might be… / One possible advantage is… / Personally, I would prefer…
